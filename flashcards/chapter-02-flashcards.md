## Chapter 2: Output Evaluation and Validation — Flashcards

| # | Front (Question) | Back (Answer) |
|---|-----------------|---------------|
| 1 | Hallucination vs outdated answer — key distinguishing test? | Ask: did this fact ever exist? Fabricated/never-true = hallucination. Once-true but since changed = outdated. Exam tip: outdated answers often pass a surface fact-check but fail a timeliness check. |
| 2 | Repeatable fact-check pass — what four things get checked independently? | Claims, numbers, names, dates. Exam tip: run all four as separate passes — don't assume catching one error type catches the others. |
| 3 | Is Claude's self-verification sufficient evidence for a high-stakes claim? | No. Self-verification only confirms internal consistency, not ground truth. Exam tip: always cross-check high-stakes claims against an external primary source. |
| 4 | Low-stakes internal draft — human review requirement? | Optional / spot-check only. Exam tip: mandatory review scales with audience exposure and consequence of error, not output length. |
| 5 | Client-facing financial/legal/medical content — human review requirement? | Mandatory full review before release. Exam tip: 'client-facing + high consequence' is the trigger phrase the exam tests for this tier. |
| 6 | Subtle bias checklist — three places to inspect | Tone (loaded language), framing (what's emphasized first/last), omission (perspectives or data left out). Exam tip: omission bias is hardest to catch because nothing on the page looks factually wrong. |
| 7 | Completeness check — what are you actually verifying? | That the answer covers the full scope of the source/question, with nothing silently dropped (e.g., 3 of 5 risks listed). Exam tip: compare the question actually asked vs what was actually answered. |
| 8 | Table vs bullets vs prose — what drives the choice? | Table = compare multiple items/metrics; bullets = scannable discrete points; prose = narrative reasoning or nuance. Exam tip: the driver is audience speed-of-consumption need, not content type alone. |
| 9 | Primary-source fact-check workflow — first step? | Identify every verifiable claim in the output, then trace each to an original/primary document before trusting it. Exam tip: a secondary summary — even one written by Claude — is never a primary source. |
| 10 | Why verify numbers and dates separately from narrative claims? | Numeric/date errors are often plausible-looking and don't trigger fluency red flags the way broken logic does. Exam tip: verify every number against source even when the sentence 'reads fine'. |
| 11 | A citation passes Claude's own self-check — correct next action? | Independently verify it in an external database/source; a passed self-check is not evidence of accuracy. Exam tip: this is the most commonly missed trap in this domain. |
| 12 | Low-stakes vs high-stakes — how should verification effort be allocated? | Match verification effort to the consequence of being wrong, not to how confident the output sounds. Exam tip: confident tone is not correlated with accuracy. |
