**Q1.** A candidate has 12 minutes remaining and sees 5 flagged questions: 2 from Output Evaluation, 1 from Data Governance, 1 from Model Monitoring, and 1 from Bias Mitigation. Time is too short to fully reconsider all 5. Which approach is BEST?

A) Answer them in the order they appear on screen
B) Skip all flagged questions and submit immediately to avoid running out of time
C) Prioritize whichever questions have the longest answer text, since those are usually harder
D) Prioritize the Output Evaluation questions first since that domain carries the highest exam weight at 21%

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

With limited time, review should be allocated proportionally to domain weight. Output Evaluation at 21% is the heaviest-weighted domain, so correcting mistakes there has the greatest impact on the scaled score.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Screen order has no relationship to scoring impact or domain weight.
- Skipping flagged questions entirely forfeits points you could still recover; an unanswered question is scored as incorrect.
- Answer length is not correlated with difficulty or domain weight and is an unreliable heuristic.

**Q2.** After finishing the exam, a candidate calculates they answered 45 of 60 questions correctly (75%) but receives a scaled score of 695, below the 720 passing threshold. What is the MOST accurate explanation?

A) The exam was scored incorrectly and should be disputed
B) The scaled score of 720 out of 1000 is not a direct percentage of raw questions answered correctly — it reflects weighted difficulty and domain performance, so raw percent correct does not map 1:1 to the scaled score
C) The candidate must have answered fewer than 45 questions correctly for the system to show that score
D) Scaled scores only apply to multiple-response items, so this discrepancy is a display bug

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

720/1000 is a scaled psychometric score, not simply 72% of 60 raw questions. Item difficulty, domain weighting, and question calibration all factor into the final scaled result, so raw percent correct and scaled score are not equivalent.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- There is no indication of a scoring error; this is expected behavior of scaled scoring.
- This assumes a false 1:1 mapping between raw correct count and scaled score.
- Scaled scoring applies to the whole exam, not just multiple-response items, and this is not a bug.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q3.** A candidate has 120 minutes for 60 questions, several of which are multiple-response. Which time budgeting approach BEST reflects recommended pacing?

A) Spend exactly 2 minutes on every question regardless of type, since the average works out evenly
B) Spend as long as needed on early questions since time can be made up later
C) Answer multiple-response questions first since they are always weighted more heavily
D) Budget roughly 2 minutes per single-response question and extra time for multiple-response questions, checking progress at set checkpoints

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

The 2-minute average must account for the fact that multiple-response items take longer to read and answer correctly, so extra time should be reserved for them while using checkpoints to stay on pace overall.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Treating every question identically ignores that multiple-response items require more reading and verification time.
- Overspending early risks running out of time for later questions with no guarantee of recovery.
- Multiple-response questions are not automatically weighted higher; question type does not equal score weight.

</details>

**Q4.** Midway through the exam, a candidate reconsiders a question they already answered confidently and starts doubting it due to time pressure, with no new information to justify a change. What is the recommended action?

A) Change the answer, since reviewing under pressure often reveals mistakes
B) Leave the original answer unless there is a specific, concrete reason to change it, and move forward to avoid wasting time
C) Flag it again and plan to revisit it three more times before submitting
D) Guess a different answer at random to diversify risk

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Second-guessing confirmed answers without new evidence under time pressure typically wastes time and often converts correct answers into incorrect ones. The recommended strategy is to trust confirmed answers absent a concrete reason to change.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Changing without new information is a common cause of turning right answers into wrong ones.
- Repeated revisits consume time budget that could go toward unanswered flagged items.
- Random guessing after already committing to a reasoned answer discards the value of the original analysis.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q5.** A candidate assumes that on a multiple-response question, selecting 3 out of 4 correct options will earn partial credit. How does this assumption typically affect performance?

B) It is accurate only for Output Evaluation domain questions

Multiple-response items typically require selecting every correct option to receive credit for that item; partial selections are scored as incorrect. This makes careful reading of the full question stem and all options critical.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Assuming partial credit leads candidates to rush or under-select, costing points unnecessarily.
- Scoring rules for multiple-response items are not domain-specific exceptions.
- The exam blueprint explicitly includes multiple-response items across domains, so this option is factually wrong.

**Q7.** A candidate reaches question 30 of 60 and checks the timer, which shows 95 minutes remaining out of the original 120. What does this indicate about their pacing?

A) They are ahead of pace, having used only 25 minutes for half the exam, leaving extra time for flagged review
B) They are exactly on pace, since half the questions and half the time should align perfectly at this point
C) They are behind pace and must rush through the remaining questions
D) Pacing cannot be assessed without knowing how many questions were multiple-response

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

At question 30 of 60, the expected elapsed time at a 2-minute-per-question average is about 60 minutes. Having 95 minutes remaining means only 25 minutes were used, putting the candidate well ahead of pace with extra buffer for flagged-question review.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Being on pace at question 30 would mean roughly 60 minutes remaining, not 95.
- 95 minutes remaining at the halfway point is ahead of, not behind, the expected pace.
- While multiple-response items affect fine-grained pacing, the checkpoint comparison against the 2-minute average is still a valid general pacing signal.

**Q8.** With 20 minutes left and 8 flagged questions spanning multiple domains, which review order BEST reflects the recommended flag-and-return strategy?

A) Review flagged questions in the exact order they were flagged, regardless of domain
B) Review only questions from domains the candidate feels weakest in, ignoring domain weight entirely
C) Skip review entirely and use the remaining time to recheck already-confirmed answers
D) Review flagged questions from the highest-weighted domains first, then move to lower-weighted domains as time allows

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Since review time is limited, allocating it proportionally to domain weight maximizes expected score gain — fixing a mistake in a 21%-weighted domain like Output Evaluation has more scoring impact than one in a lightly weighted domain.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Flag order is arbitrary and unrelated to potential score impact.
- Weak-domain feeling alone ignores the quantifiable impact of domain weight on the scaled score.
- Rechecking confirmed answers without new information risks introducing errors and ignores unresolved flagged items.

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>
